Case Studies · Three metros, same thesis

The gap travels — with local reasons and local fixes.

Below are short narratives plus a supporting data point each. All figures are illustrative sample syntheses — real source families are cited so you can audit or replace them with city open data.

Los Angeles, CA

From 31% to 12%: canopy halves across the freeway

South LA’s street grid was cut by freight rail and freeway interchanges in the 1950s–60s. Lots were paved, setbacks shrank, and the city’s tree budget followed the tax base uphill. A county canopy assessment found hillside tracts averaging ~31% cover while South LA tracts averaged ~12%. Summer surface temps tracked the gap: +7.4°C hotter on bare blocks — often the same blocks with the highest asthma rates.

Income + race: 62% Latino, 18% Black in low-canopy tracts

Phoenix, AZ

Shade is survival infrastructure

In Maricopa County, neighborhoods with <10% cover recorded 40+ excess heat-associated ER visits per 10k residents compared to >30% neighborhoods over the 2020–2023 heat seasons. A city cool-corridor pilot (shade trees + light pavement + water) cut block-level radiant temp by 4.2°C — but only on streets where neighbors co-signed maintenance.

Renters 58% in hottest tracts — shade without tenure

New York City, NY

The tree-belt and the heat-belt

MillionTreesNYC lifted citywide canopy to ~22%, but gains clustered where planting strips already existed. Community districts in the Bronx and eastern Queens still show 15–18 point gaps versus Upper Manhattan brownstone blocks. Schoolyards in the heat-belt average half the tree-equivalent shade of those in the tree-belt — an attendance gap on 90°F+ days.

Bronx CD poverty rate 28% vs Manhattan 14% — canopy maps onto it

Synthesis

Different histories, same physics.

Los Angeles (freeways), Phoenix (sprawl + heat), New York (rowhouse vs tower) each produced low-canopy tracts where lower-income residents now face hotter surfaces and worse air. The fix set is also shared: protect what exists, plant where the gap is widest, and fund maintenance — not just saplings.
